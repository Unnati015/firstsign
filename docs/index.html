FirstSign

India's early-warning network for disease outbreaks, built at the chemist's counter.

The problem

Every monsoon, dengue, malaria and typhoid spread through our neighbourhoods. The first sign appears around day two, when people start buying fever tablets, ORS and test kits at the local chemist. The health department finds out around day ten, from hospital reports. By then the disease has spread, hospitals are overwhelmed and pharmacies have run out of test kits. Outbreak: a disease spreading through a neighbourhood faster than usual.

How it works

  1. Chemist's counter (the sensor): fever tablets, ORS and test kits sold today.
  2. Billing software they already use (the wire): anonymised daily counts of a few products by pin code. No patient data. One billing app alone serves 1.75 lakh chemists.
  3. FirstSign AI (the brain): adds lab positivity and weather, learns each pin code's normal, and scores an Outbreak Index 1 to 2 weeks before official reports.
  4. Forecasts go out: to health officers, hospitals and labs, pharma and distributors. Today's pharma sales data is monthly; ours is daily.
  5. The chemist gets something back: a free forecast of what to stock next week, plus a revenue share.

Launches with no insurer and no government approval needed. Precedent: Kinsa sold ZIP-code fever data to Clorox in the US; we do it with chemists, who are already in every Indian neighbourhood.

Costs: chemist revenue share ≈ ₹2 crore; data and team ≈ ₹12 crore. Break-even around year 3.

Our weakest assumptions: (1) chemist sign-up, so the pilot starts with one city, 500 chemists through one billing-software partner, one monsoon; (2) signal accuracy, backtested against official reports and lab positivity; (3) willingness of pharma to pay for daily data; (4) privacy: aggregated product counts only, no patient data.
